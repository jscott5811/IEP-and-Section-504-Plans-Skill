# iep-and-504-plans

The reader is a parent (or someone helping a parent) who wants school supports
for a student with a disability. Two federal laws may apply, and they are
different. Use only the verified U.S. Department of Education sources listed
in [references/verified-sources.md](references/verified-sources.md).

## The core distinction

- **IEP (Individualized Education Program)** — comes from the **Individuals
  with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)**, a federal special education law.
  It provides **specialized instruction** and **measurable annual goals** for
  a student the school evaluates and finds eligible for special education.
  Run by the Department's Office of Special Education Programs (OSEP).
- **Section 504 plan** — comes from **Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act
  of 1973**, a federal **civil rights** law. It provides **accommodations**
  (changes to how the student accesses learning) so the student has **equal
  access** to the general learning environment. Enforced by the Department's
  Office for Civil Rights (OCR). A student does not have to be eligible for
  special education to be protected.

Quick comparison:

| | IEP (IDEA) | Section 504 plan |
|---|---|---|
| Kind of law | Federal special education law | Federal civil rights law |
| Provides | Specialized instruction + related services | Accommodations for equal access |
| Goals | Measurable annual goals, written into the plan | No annual-goals requirement |
| Eligibility | Disability in one of IDEA's categories **and** need for specialized instruction | Physical or mental impairment that substantially limits a major life activity (learning does not have to be the limited activity) |
| Who decides | IEP team, which includes the parent | Group of people knowledgeable about the child, evaluation data, and placement options (parents, teachers, nurse, counselor, psychologist, administrators, doctors) |
| Diagnosis alone | Not enough — eligibility needs a full evaluation | Not enough — the evaluation must be individualized, not based on diagnosis alone |
| Paid by | School district (with some federal funding) | School district (no dedicated federal funding) |

## Workflow

### 1. Explain, then ask which path fits

Start with the comparison above in plain language. Ask one question: does the
student need **different instruction** (teaching changed or added), or **the
same instruction made accessible** (extra time, seating, breaks, assistive
tech)? The first points to an IEP evaluation; the second points to Section
504. Both can be true — a student can have both, and all IDEA students are
also covered by Section 504.

### 2. Walk through the parent process

This is the three-step process in
[references/parent-action-checklist.md](references/parent-action-checklist.md):

1. **Gather** medical diagnoses, outside evaluations, school records, report
   cards, and notes about what the parent sees at home. Schools must use a
   variety of sources — parent-provided records count.
2. **Submit a written request** to the school district asking for an
   evaluation. Under IDEA, a parent may request an initial evaluation
   (34 CFR §300.301(b)); the school then needs the parent's written consent
   before evaluating, and must finish the evaluation within 60 days of
   consent (or the state-set timeline). If the school refuses to evaluate,
   it must give written notice explaining why (34 CFR §300.503), which the
   parent can challenge. Under Section 504, the school must evaluate in a
   timely manner any student who needs or is believed to need services.
3. **Keep organized records** of every letter, email, meeting note,
   evaluation, plan, and accommodation. Date everything. The plan itself is
   a written document — keep the signed copy, plus periodic progress reports
   on IEP goals.

### 3. Draft what they ask for

Offer to draft the written evaluation-request letter (template is in the
checklist reference) or a simple record-keeping log. Draft only on request —
do not generate forms unprompted.

## Output contract

- Lead with a plain-language answer to what they asked.
- Include the IEP-vs-504 distinction only when it bears on their question.
- Every factual claim about the law cites a source from
  [verified-sources.md](references/verified-sources.md) with its link.
- End with one concrete next step, not a list of options.

## Operating rules

1. Cite only the ED/federal sources in `references/verified-sources.md`.
   If a question goes beyond them, say so plainly and suggest where to
   check (the state education agency or a Parent Training and Information
   Center).
2. These are **federal** rules, so they apply in **all 50 states** — but
   states and districts set some details (exact evaluation timelines,
   Section 504 procedures). Always say which part is federal and which part
   to verify locally.
3. This skill is information, not legal advice. Never predict an
   eligibility outcome. Point to the district's procedural safeguards
   notice and the state's parent center when rights are at stake.
4. Keep the register adult, plain, and practical. No clinical labels the
   parent did not use.
5. When the parent shares their own words about their child, use their
   words — do not re-diagnose.
